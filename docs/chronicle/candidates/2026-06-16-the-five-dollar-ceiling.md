## The record

On 2026-06-16 the Founder tested the protocol's own live purchase flow and it failed. His report, preserved in the build repository in a commit dated 2026-06-16 10:53:06 UTC, opens: "I just tested the live purchase flow from 4 different wallets. The $25 package / buy membership transaction reverted."
He set the screen down as a list rather than a sentence — the approval step, "Approve USDC confirmed"; the purchase itself, "Buy Membership failed / reverted"; the amount, "USDC amount: $25"; and what the interface had promised for it, "SYN received: 2,500 SYN". The message returned to him he quoted in full: "Transaction reverted. The transaction was submitted but reverted on-chain. No funds were moved beyond gas." The instruction that followed was one line: "Debug the live Sale V2 purchase/package flow before any journey, IA, redesign, or docs work."
The amounts his report asked to be checked were $5, $10, $25, $50 and $75. Only one of them could be bought. The engine then live enforced a cumulative limit per wallet for the opening era, and that limit was five dollars — the same figure as the era's own minimum entry. The floor and the ceiling had been set to the same number, "collapsing min and max", so the era admitted exactly one five-dollar purchase per wallet, refused every larger amount, and refused every second attempt. The preparation report written that day states it without softening: "The $5 Genesis defect is LIVE. maxUsdcPerAddressPerEra(1) = 5,000,000 ($5). The headline $10k/$25k Genesis package is impossible on V2a right now."
Two properties of the engine explain why the failure arrived only at the moment of signing. The quotation the interface reads never consults that limit — it returns the tokens, the era, the rate, the seat and the inventory, and nothing about how much this wallet is allowed to spend — so a quote for twenty-five dollars is correct in every figure it gives. And the limit is checked before any money moves: the purchase reverts having spent gas and nothing else. No funds were ever at risk. The purchase was simply impossible.

## What the protocol did

The first correction was the small one, and it was the one the day's diagnosis recommended: "the lawful minimal fix is to make the UI conform to the live era cap". It was built — the purchase interface changed to display the live era rates and minimums — and committed at 12:29:17 UTC.
Eleven minutes and forty-six seconds later, at 12:41:03 UTC, the same memo was amended and that direction was reversed. The section added is titled "Founder intent SUPERSEDES the UI-clamp workaround (ratified)", and it states: "There is no remaining reason to keep the $5 per-address cap: one-seat is enforced by IDENTITY (firstSeat = !knownMember), not by dollars". Five dollars was to survive as the entry floor and nowhere else: "Keep $5 ONLY as minUsdc6 (the entry floor), never as the per-address ceiling."
Reversing it was expensive, because the limit is not a setting. It is written once, when the engine is created, and no owner function in the contract can reach it afterwards. The memo says so in one line: "There is NO setter for the cap (only constructor)." Correcting a number of that class means deploying a new engine and carrying every existing member onto it.
That is what the day's three reports prepared. The report that rehearsed the replacement's parameters names exactly four fields that differ from the canonical parameter file, and records everything else as byte-identical. The four: a fail-closed flag that made the rehearsal file refuse to deploy outside a test fork; the opening era's per-wallet limit raised from 5,000,000 to 25,000,000,000 base units — five dollars to twenty-five thousand; the count of members already seated moved from 2 to 5; and the membership commitment replaced by one derived over the buyers of both engines. The everything-else it lists is the token and treasury addresses, the eight remaining per-wallet era limits, the per-transaction ceiling, the seat reserve, all the era supply caps, and the router. The finalized file carries those three substantive values and clears the flag, and the engine that was deployed answers to all three — which is why the successor opened with the five existing members already counted, so that nobody already seated could be issued a second seat.
The reports also refused to let the correction look complete. A second and independent reason the largest package could not be sold was inventory: the live engine's "sellable headroom is 901,500 SYN ≈ $9,015" — "less than a single $10k package" — so raising the limit alone would not have made that purchase possible. And the rollback instruction carries its own cost in the same breath: re-opening the old engine restores a working sale, and "the $5 defect returns".
The replacement engine was deployed the next day. Its creation transaction was mined in block 88,193,183 at 2026-06-17T00:58:29Z. Both engines are still readable. Asked today what a single wallet may spend in the opening era, the old one answers five dollars and the new one answers twenty-five thousand.

## Why this is recorded

An immutable parameter is not a setting. It is a decision already taken, and the only way to change it is to replace the thing that carries it. A five-dollar ceiling written once into a constructor cost a new engine, a re-derived member snapshot, a fresh funding requirement, and the carrying-across of every seat that already existed — and it was found not by a test but by the Founder trying to buy a membership from four wallets and watching each attempt revert.
Two things about that day are worth keeping. The first is the correction that was itself corrected. Making the interface agree with the contract was defensible, it was implemented, and it was overturned within twelve minutes — because it would have made the protocol scrupulously honest about a limit that should never have existed. Agreeing with a mistake is not the same as fixing it, and those twelve minutes are the whole distance between the two ideas.
The second is that nothing written that day pretended the fix was the end of it. The inventory gap and the cost of rolling back sit in the same documents as the correction, in plain words — including the sentence admitting that reverting to the old engine brings the defect back with it. That is the standard this register holds itself to: a record of a repair must also carry what the repair does not cover.
